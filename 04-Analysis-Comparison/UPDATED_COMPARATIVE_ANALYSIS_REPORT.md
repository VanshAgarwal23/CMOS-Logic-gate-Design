# CMOS Logic Gate Design — Updated Comparative Analysis Report

## Inverter vs 2-Input NAND vs 2-Input NOR

**Simulation platform:** LTspice 26.0.2
**Technology model:** Level-1 NMOS/PMOS compact models
**Nominal supply:** 5 V
**Nominal output load:** 50 fF
**Verification date:** September 2026

## Executive Summary

This report provides an updated transistor-level comparison of three static CMOS logic cells implemented in the project:

1. CMOS inverter
2. 2-input CMOS NAND
3. 2-input CMOS NOR

The comparison is based on the **latest verified simulation artifacts supplied for the project**, rather than the obsolete values in the earlier comparative report.

The corrected inverter characterization uses a 5 V supply, 50 fF load, 100 ns transient window, and isolated transitions generated by a 1 µs-period pulse. Its measured propagation delays are 0.557340 ns (`tpHL`) and 0.538143 ns (`tpLH`), giving 0.547741 ns average delay. Average supply power is 13.758791 µW over the 100 ns measurement interval.

The corrected NAND uses the same 5 V supply, 50 fF load, and 100 ns transient window. Input B is held at 5 V while input A switches. It produces 0.903516 ns `tpHL`, 0.540487 ns `tpLH`, and 0.722002 ns average delay, with 13.739716 µW average power.

The supplied NOR characterization uses the same 5 V and 50 fF conditions but a 400 ns transient window and a different input period. It produces 0.537748 ns `tpHL`, 0.426757 ns `tpLH`, and 0.482253 ns average delay, with 6.633439 µW average power.

**Important comparability limitation:** propagation-delay comparisons are reasonably interpretable because all three designs use the same nominal VDD, load, transistor-model family, and 2.5 V measurement threshold. However, the **average-power and total-energy values are not directly apples-to-apples** because the input periods, duty cycles, active-input configurations, and measurement windows are not identical. The reported power values should therefore be treated as **simulation-condition-specific measurements**, not as a universal power ranking.

# 3. CMOS Topology and Electrical Consequences

## 3.1 Inverter

The inverter has a single-device pull-up path and a single-device pull-down path.

When `IN = 0`:

- PMOS is ON.
- NMOS is OFF.
- Output is charged toward VDD.

When `IN = 1`:

- PMOS is OFF.
- NMOS is ON.
- Output is discharged toward ground.

The inverter therefore provides the reference topology against which stacked CMOS gates can be understood.

## 3.2 NAND2

The NAND pull-up network is parallel:

```text
VDD
 |---- PMOS A ----|
 |                |
 |---- PMOS B ----|---- OUT
                  |
               NMOS A
                  |
               NMOS B
                  |
                 GND
```

The pull-down path contains two series NMOS devices. For the measured transition, input B is held HIGH, so when A rises both NMOS devices participate in discharging the output.

A series stack increases the effective pull-down resistance and introduces additional internal capacitance. This is the main topology-level reason why the measured NAND `tpHL` is larger than the inverter `tpHL`.

## 3.3 NOR2

The NOR pull-up network is series and the pull-down network is parallel:

```text
VDD
 |
 PMOS A
 |
 PMOS B
 |
 OUT
 |---- NMOS A ---- GND
 |
 |---- NMOS B ---- GND
```

With input B tied LOW, the active switching path is isolated to input A. The NOR therefore avoids simultaneously exercising both inputs during the delay measurement.

The 8 µm PMOS width compensates for the series PMOS topology. In a first-order resistance model, two equal-width series PMOS devices have approximately twice the resistance of one device, so doubling the width relative to the 4 µm inverter PMOS restores a similar first-order effective width.

## NAND2


tpLH / tpHL
=

The measured falling transition is substantially slower than the rising transition.

The primary topology-related factor is the **series NMOS pull-down stack**.

## NOR2


tpLH / tpHL
=
\frac{0.426757}{0.537748}
≈ 0.794


The rise transition is faster than the measured fall transition for this particular configuration.

Because the NOR PMOS devices were widened to 8 µm, the series PMOS resistance is compensated relative to an unsized series stack.

# 7. Why NAND and NOR Behave Differently

The most important transistor-level difference is the location of the series stack.

| Gate | Pull-up network | Pull-down network | Primary stacked path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inverter | 1 PMOS | 1 NMOS | None |
| NAND2 | 2 PMOS parallel | 2 NMOS series | NMOS |
| NOR2 | 2 PMOS series | 2 NMOS parallel | PMOS |

A first-order CMOS delay relationship is:


t_p ≈ 0.69R_{eq}C_L


where `Req` is the effective conducting transistor-network resistance and `CL` includes the external load plus relevant parasitic capacitance.

Therefore:

- A series network tends to increase `Req`.
- A parallel network can reduce `Req` when multiple devices conduct.
- Increasing W generally decreases channel resistance but increases gate and diffusion capacitance.
- Consequently, transistor upsizing is a speed/area/capacitance trade-off rather than a universally beneficial modification.

# 8. Power Comparison

## 8.1 Measured Average Power

| Gate | Average power | Measurement window |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inverter | 13.758791 µW | 0–100 ns |
| NAND2 | 13.739716 µW | 0–100 ns |
| NOR2 | 6.633439 µW | 0–400 ns |

The inverter and NAND measurements are numerically close.

The NOR value is lower, but **this cannot be interpreted directly as a topology-only power advantage** because the NOR uses a different input period and a 400 ns measurement window.

Dynamic CMOS power can be approximated by:


P_{dyn}≈ α C_LV_{DD}^{2}f


where:

- \(α\) = switching activity,
- \(C_L\) = effective switched capacitance,
- \(V_{DD}\) = supply voltage,
- \(f\) = switching frequency.

Since \(α\) and `f` differ between the current simulations, average power is not normalized across all three gates.

The corresponding average-energy rate is simply:


E / T=P_{avg}


For the NOR:


2.653376 pJ / 400 ns
=
6.633439\ µ W


A useful normalization for visual comparison is the equivalent energy delivered per 100 ns:


E_{NOR,100ns}=2.653376/4
=0.663344\ pJ


However, this **does not mean 0.663344 pJ is the NOR single-transition energy**. It is merely the 400 ns integrated energy scaled linearly to a 100 ns interval.

For all three designs, the current integrated-energy measurement should therefore be described as **window energy**, not single-transition switching energy.

# 10. A Fairer Power Benchmark

For a rigorous gate-to-gate power comparison, all three simulations should eventually be rerun with:

- VDD = 5 V
- CL = 50 fF
- same input transition time
- same switching frequency
- same active-input strategy
- same simulation duration
- same measurement window
- same `.meas` power expression
- same number of active transitions

An even stronger metric is **energy per output transition**:


E_{transition} =
∫_{t_1}^{t_2}P(t)dt


where `t1` and `t2` isolate one complete switching event.

This removes much of the ambiguity caused by different pulse periods and measurement windows.

## NAND2

The NMOS devices remain at 2 µm but are placed in series.

A simple resistance approximation gives:


R_{series}≈ R_1+R_2


Thus, two equal 2 µm NMOS devices create approximately twice the channel resistance of a single 2 µm NMOS, before parasitic effects are included.

## NOR2

The PMOS devices are 8 µm and connected in series.

Using the approximate relationship:


R∝\frac{1}{W}


two 8 µm PMOS devices in series give approximately:


R_{eq}∝
1/8 + 1/8
=
1/4


which is approximately equivalent, in this simplified resistance model, to a single 4 µm PMOS.

This explains the rationale for the 8 µm NOR PMOS sizing.

# 19. Technical Conclusions

### 19.1 Inverter

The inverter establishes the reference two-transistor topology with one pull-up and one pull-down device. Its measured rise/fall delays are closely matched under the selected sizing, producing an average delay of 0.547741 ns.

### 19.2 NAND2

The NAND introduces a series NMOS pull-down stack. Under the measured condition where B is fixed HIGH, this stack is directly involved in the output discharge path. The resulting `tpHL` of 0.903516 ns is approximately 1.62× the inverter `tpHL`.

The NAND `tpLH` is close to the inverter `tpLH`, because the measured rising transition uses the active PMOS pull-up path while the other PMOS is controlled by the fixed input.

### 19.3 NOR2

The NOR uses parallel NMOS devices and series PMOS devices. The PMOS width was increased to 8 µm to compensate for the series pull-up topology. Under the supplied simulation conditions, the measured average delay is 0.482253 ns.

Its measured average power is 6.633439 µW, but this should not be interpreted as a topology-only power advantage because the NOR simulation uses a different pulse period and 400 ns measurement window.

# 20. Final Comparison Statement

The updated simulations demonstrate the expected relationship between CMOS topology and propagation delay:

- **Series transistor stacks increase effective resistance and can increase propagation delay.**
- **Parallel transistor networks can provide lower effective resistance when multiple devices conduct.**
- **Transistor sizing can compensate for series resistance, but larger devices also introduce additional capacitance and area.**
- **The inverter provides the simplest two-transistor reference topology.**
- **NAND and NOR provide universal logic functionality at the cost of additional transistors and topology-dependent delay behavior.**
- **Power measurements must be normalized for switching activity before making a direct energy-efficiency comparison.**

The present data are therefore suitable for a technical project comparison of **topology, transistor sizing, propagation delay, structural complexity, and simulation-condition-specific power**, while a final normalized power ranking should be deferred until all three gates are characterized under identical switching activity.
